# 06 — Story, Substance and Memory

For pieces that are structurally sound and still dry, abstract, forgettable, or unpersuasive. This is where a competent piece becomes one worth reading.

## Question, not topic

**Most writers begin with a topic. Begin with a question instead.**

> **A topic gives you a pile of things to say. A question gives you a reason to say them.**

A topic is also bloated: "genetics" is a 500-page book, not an article. A question threads several topics into one piece.

**What a question buys you: material that would otherwise be a digression.** Driven by *why are identical twins alike, and why do they differ?*, a piece on epigenetics can include a researcher's politics during the war, a wartime famine, and an experiment on an ant caste system. None relate to the *topic*. All answer the *question*, so all stay.

Test it on your own material:

- Topic: climate policy → Wikipedia entry.
- Question: *why did a town vote against solar panels it had already paid for?* → an article.

Two practical uses when improving a draft:

1. If a section feels like a list of facts, the piece has a topic and needs a question.
2. If good material was cut as "off-topic", check it against the question before agreeing.

## Judgment

**A question forces you to make judgment calls, and that is a feature.**

> **All stories have a moral universe — an interior logic. A story without judgment is not a story.** It is a Wikipedia article: information transmitted with no evaluation.

Narrative non-fiction is not objective reporting. It is coloured by value judgments, like all good stories.

- If you have the standing, make the call in your own voice.
- If you do not, **back it with someone who does** — quote or cite a person with authority in the field.
- **Never skip the judgment to sound neutral.** Neutrality reads as having nothing to say.
- When improving someone else's draft, do not invent their judgment. If a section reads as evaluation-free, say so and ask what they actually think.

## Foreground and background

**The expert instinct is to push what you know into the foreground: define, explain, teach.** Readers do not want that. They want to be shown.

| | Put here |
|---|---|
| **Foreground** | What you have personally seen. Who you talked to. What was in the room. The prop on the desk. What their office says about them. What their face revealed. |
| **Background** | Primary research. Papers, statistics, historical context, definitions. |

**The common failure is not reversing these. It is having no foreground at all** — never leaving the desk, so the foreground holds only research and ideas.

### Teach inside a scene

When a concept is load-bearing, do not stop the story to define it. Put it in a person's hands:

> "…a chain of DNA does not typically sit naked in animal cells. It is wrapped tightly around a core of proteins called histones. To demonstrate, Allis stood up from his desk, navigated his way through the stacks of books and papers, and pointed out a model, **a long plastic tube, cerulean blue, twisted sinuously around a series of white discs, like a python coiled around a skewer of marshmallows.**"

No definition is handed over. The narrative never breaks. **The idea is not dumbed down — the reader is trusted and given an image**, and the image does more work than paragraphs of explanation.

Note also that the image is **idiosyncratic**. Very few writers would reach for a python and marshmallows; it is coloured by that writer's own experience, which is precisely why it lands. **Do not sand off an unorthodox image that is the author's own.** In Establish mode, do not manufacture one either — reach for the concrete detail the author actually supplied.

### Three sources

A well-rounded piece draws on **research, interviews, and reporting**. Most writers treat research as the important one.

> **Research and information are essentially free now. Readers can get them without you. What they cannot get is you in the room, noticing the stacks of books and the model on the desk. That is the value; the research supports it.**

**When improving a draft with no foreground, this is the single highest-value gap to report.** You cannot supply it — the author has to. Name it precisely: "Section 3 explains the mechanism but nobody is in the room. Was there a demo, a screen, a person you watched do this?"

## Structure in time

**You and the reader experience the text differently.** You have a god's-eye view — you know the story, did the research, hold the whole map. The reader walks the map one sentence at a time with no idea what is round the corner. **That blind spot is where a lot of non-fiction falls apart.**

The fix: **lay the piece out in time, because time is the dimension in which payoffs get set up.**

**Mark every jump with a plain signal** — *By the time I was born in 1970… One afternoon in April… In the fall of 2003…* Among the most boring phrases in the language, doing enormous work: they tell the reader where they are on the map.

### Payoffs

A payoff at the end is impossible without a set-up, and a set-up is impossible without orienting the reader in time.

- **Plant an image early and pay it off late.** Twins with the same freckled skin; a child scanning a face for its exact map of freckles to tell them apart. At the end, epigenetic marks "etching the genome with its own scars, calluses, and **freckles**."
- A closing metaphor that was never set up is a clever line, not a payoff. When editing, check: **is the last image earned?** If not, either plant it earlier or cut it.

## The bridge

**Assume your reader does not care about what you care about.** If they did, they would already be reading the specialist literature.

> Consider the opposite assumption. If you assume readers share your values, you are preaching to the converted — and if they already agree, what is the piece worth? **Your piece does the work of moving somebody onto your side.**

Watch for values deployed as self-evidently good — *sustainability, diversity, progress, innovation, efficiency, alignment*. Maybe they should be self-evident. To an unconvinced reader they are not, and using them as if they were signals that the piece was not written for them.

**Build the door where the reader already stands.** Readers who do not care about epigenetics do care about families, immigrants, and two women born in Delhi in 1942 — so the piece opens there. By the time you are in a lab looking at histones, the reader is already on side.

**Then import that world's vocabulary into the technical scenes.** Describing an ant colony with the word **caste** hyperlinks the whole family story and its emotional stakes into a scene about insects. One word.

> **We do not debate the reader into our point. We do not persuade them. We build a bridge from what they already care about to what we want to show them.**

## People carry the values

**Science does not happen on its own. People do science.** They run studies, ask questions, make decisions. The same is true of finance, engineering, policy and software.

**Show, don't tell, executed properly:**

- Telling: "the researcher was passionate." That is a **label**.
- Showing: "he scored an A− in his first biochemistry class, felt he had not really mastered the material, and applied to take the class again." That is a **character**.

**When editing, hunt labels.** *passionate, brilliant, dedicated, innovative, world-class, seamless, robust.* Each is a claim with the evidence removed. Replace with the specific act — or, if you do not have the act, flag it and ask.

**When interviewing, do not treat people as sources of information. Treat them as human beings pursuing their own motives.**

- "How did you end up spending your life on this?"
- "What was your most important experience?"

**These open doors that fact-gathering never will.** And the people in your story are not only the experts — patients, homeowners, students, neighbours, users, anyone who lives inside the world your question opens up.

> **If your reader can see people, they will follow you anywhere.**

## Write for memory

**We do not remember words. We remember images, human actions and sensory information.** The visual part of the brain is far older than the part that evolved for language.

Remembering is also not one activity. *Remember the capital of Indonesia* is factual recall. *Remember the first line of "Hey Jude"* plays a melody. *Remember the Mona Lisa* pulls an image. *Remember the house you grew up in* delivers dinner smells, creaking floors, the layout of rooms. *Remember the definition of truth* forces you to construct something. Each prompt reaches a different part of the reader's inner world — and the abstract ones are the most work.

Three moves:

1. **Choose concrete language and examples.** Not "I was in Jakarta" but the sights, sounds, smells and feelings a reader can attach to.
2. **Write about flesh-and-blood characters.**
3. **If you must write about an abstraction, make it the subject of a verb that tells a story.**

**When editing:** for each key idea, ask what the reader will *see*. If the answer is nothing, that idea will not survive the week — and if the author supplied no material to see, that is a gap to report, not to fabricate.

## Analysis, not description

Writing about a watch:

- **Descriptive:** "This is the minute hand. This is the second hand." Information. Useless.
- **Analytical:** how do the gears turn? What lets the hands move in sync? How does it map onto our experience of time?

> **Valuable writing does not repeat facts. It finds patterns, unpacks underpinnings, and uses the information to solve a problem the reader has. It is not information but analysis that makes writing valuable.**

A draft that summarises correctly and analyses nothing needs a different kind of work from a draft that is merely unclear. Say which one you are looking at.

## Order your examples AAB, not ABA

From comedy's rule of three. **Two similar examples first, then the contrast.**

- **ABA** — example, contrast, back to the original. The reader never grasps the core idea before it is muddied.
- **AAB** — A and A hammer the point home; only once it has landed does B challenge or deepen it. The steady build makes the twist land.
