# 04 — Paragraphs, Patterns and Key Terms

The level where most "it flows but says nothing" problems live.

## Topic and Comment

Every clause has two slots:

- **Topic** — the first meaningful element. The **doorstep** the reader stands on. The thing the sentence tells a story about.
- **Comment** — the end. The **room** the reader walks into.

> **Old, familiar information goes in the Topic. New information goes in the Comment.**

This is the same Index-Discussion shape scaled down to the clause: readers look at the Topic to find out what the unit is about, and at the Comment to find the idea developed. Everything below is an application of it.

### Proof that this is organisation, not grammar

**Hard to read:**
> "Things that happen are not what produces happiness. Good fortune and random chance don't result in it, either. Money can't buy it, and power can't command it. Outside events do not determine it. Our interpretation of those events does. Preparation, cultivation, and private defense by each person are what this condition requires."

**Easy to read (Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow*):**
> "Happiness is not something that happens. It is not the result of good fortune or random chance. It is not something that money can buy or power command. It does not depend on outside events, but rather on how we interpret them. Happiness, in fact, is a condition that must be prepared for, cultivated, and defended privately by each person."

Strip the topics. First version: *things that happen · good fortune · money · outside events · preparation.* Second: *happiness · happiness · happiness · happiness · happiness.* **There are no grammar mistakes in the first version.** The difference is entirely organisation.

## Cohesion is not coherence

- **Cohesion** — the pieces fit together, sentence to sentence.
- **Coherence** — the pieces make one picture.

A passage can be perfectly cohesive and completely incoherent:

> "Soon Portland will be the coffee roasting hub of the nation. The aroma of roasting beans fills the streets and gleaming espresso machines line the counters. The machines remind me of my dad's old typewriter covered with keys I would press randomly. His typewriter always jammed on me. That's why I avoided it. I prefer to write with a blue pen on yellow legal pads. This preference has been the topic of heated debates between me and my creative writing professor."

Every handoff works. Three faults remain:

1. **Unrelated subjects** — *Portland, the aroma, the machines, his typewriter, I, this preference.*
2. **No shared theme.** A theme is a **web of closely related topics**. Around *coffee* you expect *cup, drink, conversation*. You do not expect *typewriter, preference, professor* — not impossible, just not the same ecosystem of words.
3. **No thesis** unifying the passage.

**The same-stage test:** could these elements stand together on one stage as characters in one play? What are they doing together?

## The three-step repair

1. Underline the first seven or eight words of every sentence, stopping at the verb.
2. Ask: do they form a small set of topics naming related ideas — and will a **reader** see the relation? Do they name the most important characters, real or abstract?
3. If not, rewrite so **most sentences use their subject to name the topic, placed near the beginning.**

**Before:**
> "A clear visual hierarchy at the top of web pages helps visitors identify what a site is primarily for. A sense of usability emerges when a series of menu items reflects a limited set of core functions. But the purpose of each page is obscured by apparently arbitrary changes of navigation. Confusing websites result when that happens."

**After:**
> "**Visitors** identify what a site is primarily for when they see clear visual hierarchy at the top of web pages. **They** feel a site is usable when they encounter a series of menu items that reflects a limited set of core functions. But when navigation seems to change arbitrarily, **visitors** lose track of each page's purpose. When that happens, **they** feel they are using a website that is confusing or even poorly designed."

Concrete characters now occupy the sentence openings. Consistent topics let readers understand what the passage is about globally — that is coherence. Random topic shifts produce lost context.

**Do not vary topics for variety.** The advice to avoid repetition is sentence-level advice that destroys passage-level coherence. Repeating the topic is what shows readers what the passage is about.

**Make topics short, concrete and familiar.** Nobody can picture "the more general abilities to process information or behave intelligently." Clear writing is visual — it activates the mind's eye.

## Key Terms and thematic strings

**The Index announces Key Terms. The Discussion threads them through every sentence. Readers experience that thread as coherence.**

Threads are built from **repetition, synonyms and pronouns**, and a good passage runs two or three in parallel. Frazer, on fire and rain, runs three at once:

| PEOPLE | FIRE | STOPPING RAIN |
|---|---|---|
| the Arabs | fire | stopping rain |
| the Sulka | red hot | cease to fall |
| the Telugas | burned | drive away rain |
| medicine-men | burning wood | stop rain |
| the Anula | fire-sticks | |

**Working method:** before drafting a section, name its two or three strings. After drafting, check every sentence in the Discussion touches at least one. A Key Term announced in the Index and then dropped is a broken promise — either develop it or stop announcing it.

## The four paragraph patterns

Pick by the job the paragraph has to do.

| The paragraph must… | Pattern | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Elaborate on one thing | **Constant Theme** | Stagnation |
| Move — process, causal chain | **Linking** | Drifting off topic |
| Break a whole into parts | **Super Theme** | A missing index clause |
| Weave several strands | **Theme Preview** | Synonyms instead of exact terms |

### 1. Constant Theme (also: Focus, anchored topic)

The same element occupies the Topic of every clause — by name, pronoun, or synonym. All new information develops in the Comment. The reader stands on one doorstep and looks in different directions.

**Use for:** describing, characterising, unpacking a multifaceted idea. The reader never gets lost in detail because they always know what the detail is *about*.

**Cost:** it feels **stagnant**, because the reader is standing still. Reserve it for moments that genuinely need unpacking; do not run a whole piece on it.

*Model: the Csikszentmihalyi passage above.*

### 2. Linking (also: Momentum)

Each Topic picks up the element at the **end of the previous clause**. Old → new → that new becomes old → next new. The reader steps from room to room rather than teleporting.

**Use for:** process, sequence, travel, method, any causal chain.

**Model (Gleick, *Chaos*):**
> "Outside his window, Lorenz could watch real weather. The early morning **fog** creeping along the MIT campus or the low **cloud** slipping over the roofs from the Atlantic. **Fog and clouds** never arose in the model running on his **computer**. **The machine**, a Royal McBee, was a thicket of wiring and vacuum tubes that occupied an ungainly portion of **Lorenz's** office."

Note how it ends where it began. Closing the loop is not required, but it proves you stayed on the map.

**Cost:** it works like railroad tracks and will happily carry you off course — this is exactly how the Portland passage above was built. Run the same-stage test on any linked passage.

### 3. Super Theme (also: Simplification, index clause, umbrella)

**This one breaks the repetition rule.** The Topics are all *different* elements and it still reads easily, because the **whole** was introduced in the Comment of the first clause and every following Topic is one of its parts.

Walk into a cathedral and you are not surprised to find the nave, the crypt and the pulpit. **Once the whole is on stage, the reader is not surprised to meet its parts.**

**Model (Pinker, *The Language Instinct*):**
> "When neuroscientists look directly at the brain … they can actually see language in action in **the left hemisphere**. **The anatomy of the normal brain**, its bulges and creases, is slightly asymmetrical. **In some of the regions associated with language**, the differences are large enough to be seen with the naked eye. **Aphasics' brains** almost always show lesions in the left hemisphere."

**The failure — a missing index clause.** These sentences are disjointed:
> "After gathering the smallest and most colorful leaves from the maples and oaks in our backyard, we place the leaves between sheets of blotter paper… we put one leaf on each card, leaving room for the guest's name."

Add one sentence and everything snaps into place:
> "**Each Thanksgiving we make place cards decorated with pressed autumn leaves.** After gathering…"

**Rule: if your topics name varying elements, name the unifying category explicitly, where readers expect to find it. An implied theme is not a theme.**

### 4. Theme Preview (also: Synthesis)

The Index's Comment sets up A, B and C. Each following clause or section develops A, then B, then C, **in that order**. A tour guide saying "we will visit this room, that room, and this other room."

**Model (Haidt, *The Righteous Mind*):**
> "…three major clusters of moral themes, which they called the ethics of **autonomy, community, and divinity**. The ethic of **autonomy** is based on… The ethic of **community** is based on… The ethic of **divinity** is based on…"

**The failure — synonyms.** It snaps together because the later clauses echo the **exact** key terms. *autonomy → autonomous* and *community → communal* are fine, they are the same term. *community → groups* breaks it: the reader is already tracking three elements and now has to hold a mapping too. **Lexical consistency is the whole mechanism.**

## Flow between sentences

**End each sentence with something the next can pick up.** Sentences are relay runners; each takes the baton where the last left it.

Worked example — which fills the gap?

> "Some promising results about viral growth have been unlocked by venture capitalists who harness **network effects**. ______ So many users concentrated into so little an environment creates exponential value."

- **A (active):** "The concentration of active users into a community perhaps no larger than a college campus triggers a network effect."
- **B (passive):** "**Network effects** are triggered by the concentration of active users into **a community perhaps no larger than a college campus**."

**B wins.** It opens on *network effects* — where the previous sentence ended — and closes on *a community no larger than a college campus*, which sets up the sentence after it. The passive voice is what makes both links possible. **Stop thinking in rules and think in tools.**

## Mixing patterns

Patterns nest. Run a Theme Preview across a whole section, then use Linking inside the paragraphs of part A and a Constant Theme paragraph where one entity needs unpacking. Old-to-new holds at every level.
