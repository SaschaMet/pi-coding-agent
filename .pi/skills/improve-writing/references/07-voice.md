# 07 — Voice

Read this when the gate set voice mode to **Establish** or **Match**. In **Preserve** mode, read only the first two sections and then leave the voice alone.

## What a voice actually is

> **A voice is a combination of identifiable attributes: word choice, cadence and rhythm, structure and format, even the decision to use or not use data to back an opinion.**

It is not magic and not the property of gifted stylists. **Usually it comes down to one or two clearly identifiable attributes.** To reproduce a voice you do not recreate a whole person — you identify those one or two attributes and reproduce them.

This is what makes voice work tractable. Name the attributes explicitly before you write, and check against them after.

## Where voice comes from

> **You never have writer's block when you are talking to somebody, and you never worry that your speaking voice is flat.**

When you speak to someone you respect and want respect from, your words do two jobs at once: they say the thing, and they reflect your relationship with that person. You show respect, notice what interests them, stop yourself rambling, take turns. Those cues pass almost invisibly, and **every one of them is upstream of the language.**

> **Every writer's choice is downstream of your relationship to the person you are writing to.**

The operational consequence: **before drafting, name one specific reader.** Not a segment, a person. Flat, ownerless prose is almost always prose written to nobody. If a draft feels voiceless and the structure is fine, the missing thing is usually the reader, not the vocabulary.

## The three modes

### Preserve

The draft already sounds like someone. Fix structure and information order; leave register, vocabulary, idiom and rhythm alone.

**Specifically do not:** flatten their vocabulary, remove a deliberate crude word, standardise their sentence rhythm, replace their metaphor with a safer one, or delete an unorthodox image that is theirs. An idiosyncratic image is a gift only that writer could have given.

**A useful signal:** if a change would make the piece sound like it could have been written by anyone, it is the wrong change.

### Establish

The input is notes, bullets, or a transcript. **There is no voice yet, so you have to add one** — and doing that by default produces the flat prose nobody wants to sign.

**Start from the default register.** Clear, concise, declarative, straightforward. This is right about 80% of the time, and it is not a fallback — it is the base every other voice is a small modification of. Do not reinvent the wheel; write it clearly, then turn the dials.

**Then decide, explicitly:**

1. Who is the one reader?
2. Which one or two attributes carry this voice?
3. Which archetype below is closest?

**Say which choice you made in your report**, so the author can correct it in one line instead of rewriting the piece.

### Match

Writing as or for a specific person, or to a house style.

**Work from samples, and prefer a transcript.** Writing a voice from a blank page means inventing it out of thin air. A transcript is different:

- It is **90% approved language** — words that person actually used.
- When someone says something perfectly, you do not have to recreate it.
- It puts the emphasis on their insight rather than on your performance.

> With a transcript, **the job is not to write. It is to remove, edit, cut down, organise.**

**The transcript method:**

1. **Cut the intro and outro.** The first five or ten minutes of any call are rarely productive.
2. **Cut the rambling stretches.** There will be a few.
3. **Cut strings of tiny cluttering words** and the borderline-incoherent sentences. You are not losing content.
4. **Skim for the theme.** With 70% of the clutter gone this is easy. Watch for trigger phrases: "something I don't hear a lot of people talk about" signals an opinion piece.
5. **Prep the page.** Working title, then subheads that skeleton the piece. Extract the three to five main points and paste the relevant transcript material under each.
6. **Whittle each section down.**

## The five archetypes

Most people are one of these, and each is produced by a small, specific change to clear declarative prose.

### 1. The Storyteller

Gives dates, times, locations, descriptions and arcs to drive a point home. Listen for: *"I remember…" "Back when I was…" "A few years ago…" "The first time I…"*

**How to write it:** these openers are the triggers they recognise as their own voice. But **a story is rarely interesting in itself — what makes it interesting is being used as an example that drives home the point.** So inside each section, first half story, second half lesson. That is what lets them tell their stories without making the piece about them.

### 2. The Opinionator

Less interested in telling stories than in using them to prove they are right. Listen for: *"There's a reason why…" "It's unbelievable how…" "Something most people don't understand is…"* Talks in extremes; *never* and *always* are foundational.

**How to write it: adverbs.** *really, rarely, honestly, seriously, obviously, extremely, unfortunately.* **Adverbs are emphasis, and an opinion is a thought with emphasis.** Clear declarative prose becomes opinionated by adding them.

Note the tension with `05-sentences.md`, which says cut hedges: correct in Preserve mode, wrong here. This is a dial, not a rule.

### 3. The Fact Presenter

Avoids fluff, will not share a "naked opinion", wants data behind claims.

**How to write it: remove adverbs, add data.** Golden rule: **at least one source per section.** Then they see themselves in the writing.

Three sub-types: one wants *something* credible-looking; one wants only peer-reviewed work; one wants data that tells a story.

Practical notes: put the "according to…" at the start, middle, or end of a section; hyperlink the "according to X" phrase, not the whole sentence; avoid stacking sources back to back, especially in one paragraph; if you must stack them, keep them on one topic and go rapid-fire; **one great statistic beats five mediocre ones.**

**In this skill, never invent the source.** If the register needs data the author has not supplied, flag it: "this section needs one source — do you have one, or should I mark it TK?"

### 4. The Frameworker

Talks in steps. *First we did this, then this, finally this.*

**How to write it:** crystallise the steps into a framework, **name it**, and tell the reader how many steps it has. Named things feel like real things, and readers want ideas organised, not a pile of ideas.

- **Do not name it something clever.** Put the goal in the name: *The Accountable Manager Checklist*. Clever is the enemy.
- **One named framework per piece.** No frameworks nested inside named frameworks.

### 5. The F-Bomber

Believes standing out means abrasive language.

**How to write it — two variants.** *Uncensored*: roughly one crude word per section. *Censored*: same tone with milder words, built from small intensifiers and ranting phrasing — *just, really, seriously, I have a hard time believing.*

- **One crude word per section is enough.** Crude language is associated with a type of person; once you play the card, the association is made. Repeat it and it turns annoying fast.
- **"Tone it down" rarely means change the tone.** It means keep the tone, use milder words.
- **When in doubt, write like a rant** and let the phrasing carry it rather than the profanity.

## The composite default

When you must establish a voice and have no other instruction, this combination is one almost anyone would put their name on:

- **A story** — a real example of what the reader is about to learn.
- **An opinion** — a claim with enough conviction to be worth disagreeing with.
- **A relevant study or statistic** — one, chosen well. Only if the author supplied it.
- **A named framework** — something actionable the reader can execute.
- **Optionally**, one or two sentences that push slightly.

Deliver that, then let the author tell you which parts to turn up or down. **Aim for a 95/100 first draft, not a safe one.**

## Register and vocabulary

**Forget "write at a fifth-grade reading level."** That advice assumes language only transmits information. **Language is also social**, and word choice signals your relationship to the material and to the reader.

- *"I have a prior that the election will be close"* carries the same information as *"I have an assumption"* — and also signals membership in a community that values statistics.
- *stochastic* and *random* both mean unpredictable; *stochastic* frames the idea from within a discipline.

> **If you always write at a fifth-grade level, what are you saying about how you think of your readers?**

Both directions are available. Do not fear a word that sends a reader to the dictionary; do not fear a cheap or blunt word either, if it is the one that lands for this audience.

> **There are no universal rules for writing because there are no universal readers. Writers do not have general audiences. They have specific readers.**

The constraint that does hold: **topic positions stay short, concrete and familiar** (`04-paragraphs.md`). Register lives in the Comment, not the doorstep.

## Read it aloud

**The single best stress test.** Two questions at every stumble:

1. **Can I say this in fewer words?**
2. **Would this voice say this word, phrase, or story?**

If a phrase sounds off to you, it will sound off to the author.

## What never gets outsourced

The reason all of the above matters more than it looks:

**Style and substance are not two layers.** You cannot paint a voice over an idea that is not there. Prose that is fluent with nothing underneath is the specific failure readers learn to distrust — polished and hollow, like memorised phrases in a language you cannot actually speak: fine until someone asks a question back.

So when you work on someone's draft:

- **The vision stays theirs.** What have they seen that they know to be true and want the reader to see? If that is missing, no structural work fixes the piece, and you should say so.
- **Fluency is not the goal.** A piece that reads smoothly and says nothing has been made worse, not better.
- **When the register demands substance that is absent — a story, a statistic, a scene, a stake — name the gap.** A named gap is worth more to the author than a confident invention.
