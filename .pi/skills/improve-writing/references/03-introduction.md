# 03 — Introduction, Point and Value

Where value is created or lost. Most weak non-fiction is weak here, and no amount of sentence work compensates.

## The value rule

> **Readers perceive writing as valuable when it solves a problem they care about.**

- Clear and useless is useless. Organised and useless is useless. Persuasive and useless is useless.
- Readers do not care about clarity, organisation or persuasion. Those are vehicles.
- **Nobody buys because something is new, and nobody buys because you worked hard on it.** They engage when they perceive value, and value is a solution to a problem.
- **In non-fiction, the problem matters more than the idea. If readers cannot perceive a problem, they cannot perceive value.**

A motivated reader also reads more attentively, understands more, and **experiences the writing as clearer than it is**. Motivation is not decoration on top of clarity; it is part of clarity.

## The problem statement

Fill this in before writing or revising anything. It does not go in the piece — it is your delete rule.

> **I am writing about \_\_\_\_\_ (topic) because I want to find out \_\_\_\_\_ (question) in order to help my reader \_\_\_\_\_ (gain a benefit / avoid a consequence).**

Anything in the draft that does not correspond to it comes out. If you cannot fill it in, you do not yet have a piece — you have a topic, and a topic produces a Wikipedia entry.

## From stability to instability

> **Readers trade attention for value, and value is the solution to a problem. But a stable reader has no problem to solve. So the job of the introduction is to move readers from stability to instability.**

## The six slots

| Block | Slot | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Stability** | Prelude | Optional opening move — the scene, the fact, the story. |
| | **Common Ground / Status Quo** | The general context most readers take for granted; the belief your Point displaces. |
| | **Concession** | What is right about that belief. |
| **Problem** | **Destabilizing Condition** | What is wrong, incomplete, outdated or inappropriate about it. Signal with *But* or *However*. |
| | **Consequence** | What it costs this reader to leave it unresolved. |
| **Solution** | **Point** | Your central claim — the way out of that cost. |

### Common Ground

Open with the general context of the problem that most readers take for granted. This is what makes them take the problem seriously rather than treating it as your private concern.

### Invert your Point

**Value is a change in the reader's head, so your Point is only worth something relative to what it displaces.**

- The Status Quo is what it displaces.
- **Find it by inverting your Point**, then stating that inversion **in your reader's own words**, sincerely enough that they nod in agreement.
- Example: Point = "online education beats in-person." Status Quo = "in-person education is better than online."

If you cannot write a status quo that a real person would agree with, your Point is not displacing anything and the piece has no value to deliver.

### Be fair

**Do not dismiss the Status Quo out of hand.** Grant what is right, helpful, productive, or at least understandable about it. Do not come off the top ropes swinging — "everyone who thinks this is an idiot" loses the reader you were trying to move. **Conceding first earns you the trust to upset it next.**

### But…

Now upset it. Show what is wrong, incomplete, outdated or inappropriate. Signal the turn with **But** or **However** — a plain word doing heavy work: the reader is now alert, and on your side, because you proved you understood them first.

### State the cost

**Instability alone is not a problem.** The reader must perceive the cost of leaving it unresolved, or the benefit of resolving it.

> This is why you must write for **specific** readers. To establish the value of your Point you have to know what this reader is afraid of paying or wants to gain. "Readers in general" have no fears.

**If you do not know the cost, ask. Do not invent one** — a fabricated stake is the fastest way to lose a reader who knows the subject better than the guess.

### Make your Point

The Point is what you want readers to walk away believing. State it **after** showing that what they believe is wrong and that being wrong costs them. **Its value is the distance it moves them from where they began.**

### The Solution's borrowed value

The Solution offers a way out of the cost imposed by the Problem, which means **your piece is worth exactly as much as the problem it relieves.** Weak Point, strong problem — still valuable. Strong Point, no problem — worthless. Spend your effort accordingly.

## Hooks do not work (with one exception)

**Readers do not value catchy openings. They value the solution to a relevant problem.** A startling fact, a quote, or a story works only when it **vividly sets up that problem**. A hook that is merely interesting buys you one sentence and then costs you trust.

## Reader awareness levels

Adapt the introduction to where this reader stands:

| Reader | What the introduction must do |
|---|---|
| Knows the problem, wants a solution | Confirm the problem fast, get to the Point |
| Knows the solution, wants a provider | Establish that you can deliver it |
| Does not know there is a problem | Spend most of the introduction building the problem |

## Introduction patterns to kill

> **A good introduction fails to repel.**

These three shapes repel, and they are the default in professional and academic writing:

- **Background → Thesis.** Context for its own sake, then a claim nobody was waiting for.
- **General → Specific.** Zooming in from the universe. The reader has no reason to follow the zoom.
- **Gap → Niche.** "Little research exists on X." A gap in the literature is not a problem in the reader's life.

## The Bilbo moment

Gandalf does not throw Bilbo into chaos. He visits, secures a commitment, and gives him time to get comfortable first.

Before the abstract argument, **build trust with something clear, simple and concrete that the reader can nod along to.** A book arguing for spirituality without religion opens with a teenage camping trip. A book about cryptocurrency opens with the author learning he is months from becoming a father — nothing to do with crypto, everything to do with the frame the reader will read the stakes in.

**Then structure examples simple → complex, literal → metaphorical.** Guide the reader from familiar territory into the harder terrain.

## The engine

Point at the one moment in the opening that **raises the questions the rest of the piece answers** — the first domino. *Will he take the money? Whose is it? Will he get away with it?*

If you cannot point at it, the piece has an introduction but no drive.

## Conclusion

Return to the opening problem, show it is settled, and link its resolution to a broader question. A conclusion that summarises what the reader just read adds nothing; a conclusion that closes the problem and opens a horizon earns the last paragraph.

## Titles

**Be clear, not clever.** Avoid cryptic titles and inside jokes — the title sets the context in which everything after it is interpreted, so it has to work before the piece is read, not after.

Use one of four curiosity triggers:

1. Pose a question or a puzzle.
2. Open a sequence of events with an unknown resolution.
3. Violate expectations.
4. Signal that others know something the reader does not.

Practical title assembly — decide each of these, then draft several and choose the one that serves the reader most:

- **Who** it is for · **What** they get, and how many · **Why** they should care (the outcome).
- Categories that work: the list · the story · the opinion · the question · the framework.
- The end of a title is the **promise**. "7 ways to cook healthy lunches" is fine; "…lose weight, and give up fast food for good" is better.
- Say how the reader should **feel** where feeling is the point.
- Together these create a **curiosity gap**: tell the beginning and the end of the story, not the middle.
