# 05 — Sentences

Line editing. Do this **after** structure and paragraphs, because a clean sentence in the wrong slot is still wrong.

## Every sentence tells a story

Forget macro story structures for a moment. **Every sentence tells a story, even in technical and academic prose, and every story is about someone doing something** — characters and actions.

| | Text |
|---|---|
| **Unclear** | "Once upon a time, a climb up the beanstalk was undertaken on the part of Jack. When the giant's discovery of him took place, resulting in his fright." |
| **Clear** | "Once upon a time Jack climbed up the beanstalk, when the giant discovered him and frightened him." |

The characters are Jack and the giant. In the first version they are buried in prepositional and noun phrases while *climb* and *discovery* hold the spotlight.

### Rule 1: main characters in the subject position

**The subject position is centre stage in a musical, the middle of the camera frame.** It is what receives the audience's attention. Put the people there.

### Rule 2: key actions in verbs

Two painters can look at the same bowl of fruit: one renders every detail, one fragments it into planes. **You are choosing how to construe the action.** Construe it as a noun and you force a flabby verb — *was undertaken*, *took place*, *resulted in*. Construe it as a verb and the story tells itself.

**The someone goes in the subject. The something-they-are-doing goes in the verb.**

Abstractions are allowed as characters — *the market*, *the algorithm*, *the policy* — as long as they can plausibly perform the verb. If you must write about *truth* or *justice*, **turn the abstraction into a visual character by making it the subject of a verb that tells a story.**

### Rule 3: nominalizations on purpose only

*investigate → investigation. respond → response. brave → bravery.* Called **zombie nouns** because they suck the life out of the verb.

They are the main culprit of unclear writing — and English evolved them for four good reasons. Language is an organism; it does not keep an organ that serves no purpose. Keep a nominalization when it:

1. **Links back to the previous sentence from the subject position.** "Watson and Crick discovered the structure of DNA." → "**Their discovery**…" One noun carries the whole previous clause. This is how cohesive flow gets built.
2. **Replaces *the fact that* / *the idea that*.** "The fact that he solved the puzzle surprised me" → "His solution to the puzzle surprised me" → best: "He surprised me when he solved the puzzle."
3. **Turns a clause into a concrete object.** "I approved what he proposed" → "I approved his proposal." The verb *approve* needs an object, and *his proposal* is more concrete than a clause.
   **The limit: nominalizations work as objects, not as subjects.** "His proposal for funding arrived after the deadline" is awkward — proposals do not arrive, people do. → "He proposed funding after the deadline."
4. **Names a concept so familiar to this reader that it functions as a character.** "The greenhouse effect was a topic in recent conferences" is fine for readers who follow climate science.

**The skill is telling apart the nominalization that hides an important action from the one that is simply familiar.** "There is a need for an examination of the marketing strategy" holds three: *need*, *examination*, *marketing*. The first two name actions and become verbs — "**We need to examine the marketing strategy**" — while *marketing* stays, because it is furniture to this reader.

## The runway

**Readers want complexity at the end of a sentence, not the beginning.** A plane needs a clear runway to build speed; with momentum it handles turbulence. Readers who get through the first nine or ten words easily can carry a lot afterwards.

| | Text |
|---|---|
| **Hard** | "Statistical and demographic examination of public school enrollment and funding patterns over the past five decades to identify trends in educational inequality is the focus of this research." |
| **Easy** | "In this research, we examine public school enrollment and funding patterns over the past five decades using statistical and demographic methods to identify trends in educational inequality." |

Version 1 forces the reader to decode technical terms while hacking through a **22-word subject**. Version 2 clears subject and verb in five words.

**Standard shape:** short introductory phrase → short concrete subject → verb expressing a specific action → then the sentence can run for lines if it is well built.

**Two things belong at the end:**

1. Long and complex phrases and clauses.
2. New information, especially unfamiliar technical terms.

**General principle: simple → complex, old → new.**

### The locomotive

When a sentence carries a lot of detail, put the subject and verb right up front. That core clause is the locomotive; everything else hitches on behind it.

- **Clear:** "**The entire world changed** in the few short hours between when John F. Kennedy went to bed on October 15th, 1962, and when he woke up the following morning."
- **Buried:** "Anyone who is concentrated so deeply that a flash of insight or inspiration suddenly visited them knows stillness." — fifteen words between *anyone* and *knows*.

### Long introductory clauses
>
> "Since most startup companies pivot their business models at least once during their early years, first-time entrepreneurs who are not certain about the direction of their venture should not commit all their resources to a single strategy."

Move it to the end or split it out:
> "First-time entrepreneurs should not commit all their resources to a single strategy if they are not certain about the direction of their venture, because most startup companies pivot their business models at least once during their early years."

### Keep the verb near its object
>
> "We must cultivate, if we are to inspire future leaders, a deep understanding of community engagement."

The interrupting clause hides that *cultivate* attaches to *a deep understanding*. Move the interrupter to the front or the back — **and choose which by what the next sentence starts with**, so the handoff still works:

- Next sentence starts with *communities* → "**If we are to inspire future leaders,** we must cultivate a deep understanding of community engagement. **These communities**…"
- Next sentence starts with *leaders* → "We must cultivate a deep understanding of community engagement **if we are to inspire future leaders**. **These leaders**…"

## Rhythm

Good writing moves. It shifts between fast and slow, brief and detailed, and that contrast creates momentum. Uniform sentence length reads as work whatever the content. After fixing the mechanics, read the passage aloud and listen for a stretch where every sentence is the same length — then break one.

This is not "vary your sentence length" as a rule to obey. It is a check you run with your ears.

## Devices, used once

Reach for these where a piece needs a spike, and **never more than one or two per piece**. Overused, they read as performance.

- **Alliteration** — repeated initial sounds make a phrase feel true even when it is nonsense (*curiosity killed the cat*). Powerful in a closing line.
- **Polyptoton** — one word in different parts of speech. *"It is the tortured who turn into torturers."*
- **Antithesis** — X is Y; not-X is not-Y. Take something ordinary and flip it. *"Journalism is unreadable, and literature is not read."* This is the one place a semicolon is clearly right: the two halves are one coin, not two sentences.
- **Diacope** — a word returning after a brief interruption. *"Bond. James Bond."* Most good one-liners.
- **Epistrophe** — the same phrase ending successive clauses. Like a chorus, it holds the core message while you add detail.
- **Prolepsis** — open with a pronoun that points forward, not back. *"They fuck you up, your mum and dad."* Readers cannot tolerate the ambiguity; it opens a loop they need closed.
- **Congeries** — piling up images to create an immersive, almost overwhelming effect. Works for establishing a world in an opening.
- **Enallage** — a deliberate grammatical "mistake" to make a moment strange. *"Do not go gentle into that good night."* Only when you can see exactly what it buys.
- **Ironic juxtaposition** — two things side by side so each changes how the other reads.
- **Synesthesia** — describe one sense with another. *"She smells the way the Taj Mahal looks by moonlight."* Smell connects directly to memory and emotion, which is why these lines stick.
- **Ladder of abstraction** — move deliberately between the concrete (things you can stub a toe on) and the abstract (freedom, greatness). Stay high and the writing floats; stay low and it reads as inventory.

## Cut

- **Clichés.** A cliché is a shortcut that substitutes for thought — a dying metaphor kept only because it saves the trouble of inventing a phrase. Where you find one, there is usually an unexamined idea underneath.
- **Hedges and filler** — *very, really, quite, basically, essentially, in order to, it is important to note*. In Preserve mode trim what dulls; in Establish mode see `07-voice.md`, where some of these are deliberate dials.
- **Understate the serious.** With heavy material, restraint outperforms sensation: it forces the reader to fill in the worst themselves. **Understate the serious, overstate the light.** Match tone to the weight of the subject.
