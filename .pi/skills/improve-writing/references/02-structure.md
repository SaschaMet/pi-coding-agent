# 02 — Structure

The structural model, from the whole piece down to the clause. Fix problems here before touching sentences: a beautifully written paragraph in the wrong place is still in the wrong place.

## The one structure

> **Every unit of discourse — clause, paragraph, section, essay — is an Index followed by a Discussion.**

- **Index** — a short opening that states the unit's point and names its Key Terms.
- **Discussion** — the longer, more complex part that develops what the Index promised.

**This structure is fixed, like Subject-Verb-Object.** Readers perceive it intuitively and use the Index to interpret the Discussion **whether you intend it or not**. That is the leverage: you are not imposing a format, you are filling a slot the reader is already reading for.

| Level | Index | Discussion |
|---|---|---|
| Simple sentence | short subject | longer predicate |
| Complex sentence | short main clause | longer supporting clause |
| Paragraph | 1–3 opening sentences | the development |
| Section | short opening paragraph | the body of the section |
| Whole piece | short introduction | the body |

**Index size varies** — a single line to several paragraphs, depending on the problem that unit sets up. Within one paragraph, one sentence is usually enough. Use two or three when you are writing about something complex, opening with an opposition (*X. However, Y…*), or posing a question (*X. Why X? As we will see, Y…*).

**The Index often closes with its Key Terms** — the words naming the unit's core concepts. Readers then expect those exact words developed through the Discussion. → `04-paragraphs.md`

## The skim test

> **Read only the Index position of each paragraph. If that skeleton outlines the whole story, your structure works.**

This is the single most useful structural check, and the reason it works is that it does not depend on your memory of what you meant. Run it on every draft, and run it again after restructuring.

## Writing in fractals

> **All whole structures have parts. All parts mirror the structure of the whole.**

**A section is a small essay.** Its Index states the section's Point, and **that Point does two jobs at once**:

1. It controls the paragraphs in its own Discussion.
2. It supports the Point of the unit above it.

If a section's Point does only the first job, the piece has parts but no argument. If it does only the second, the section has no internal spine and its paragraphs will drift.

Build downward: whole piece → sections → paragraphs, each with its own Index and Discussion. Then check upward: does each part's Point support the one above it?

## Where the Point goes

**Point first.** Writing seems clearest when the Point comes early in the Index structure — at the end of the beginning. Default to this.

**Point last.** If you do not want to state it up front, use the Index to set it up and put the Point **at the end of the Discussion** — not in the middle, where readers will miss it.

**Point once, not twice.** Make a unit's Point at the beginning or at the end. Not both.

> **The most common structural fault is accidental Point-last: the Index fails to give readers the themes they need, and readers cannot find the Point at all.** Watch for it whenever the skim test produces a skeleton of details.

## The three kinds of structure

Useful for naming what is wrong when a piece feels opaque.

**Private structure — the fault.** Organised by the writer's own associations. Perfectly clear to them, opaque to everyone else, because the reader was not in the room when the idea arrived. Its signature is heavy meta-discourse: the writer doing two jobs at once, expressing the idea and manually patching every logical gap. The trap is that once a piece is organised this way you have only two options — abandon the reader, or spend the whole text explaining connections that are invisible to them.

**Contextual structure — use it.** The shape readers already expect from the genre, brought with them from every previous example. Non-fiction: introduction then body. A scientific report: introduction, methods, findings, discussion. A decision memo, a release note, a landing page each have their own. Name the genre, write out the sequence its readers expect, and fill it.

**Textual structure — use it.** A pattern the text itself supplies, needing no background knowledge. This is the most portable kind, because it does not depend on what this particular reader happens to know. The Index-Discussion structure and the four paragraph patterns are textual structures.

## Levels of resolution

Patterns nest. Announce three themes at the opening of a section, give several paragraphs to each, and use ordinal signposts so the reader can track progress. Inside that macro structure, run whichever paragraph pattern each paragraph needs. Old-to-new holds at every level.

## Restructuring procedure

1. Run the skim test on the current draft and write down what the skeleton actually says.
2. Write the skeleton you want: the Point, then the sections that support it, each as a single sentence.
3. Diff the two. Missing sections are gaps; extra material that serves neither is cut.
4. For each section, draft the Index first — state the point, name the Key Terms.
5. Decide Point-first or Point-last per section.
6. Move existing prose under the right Index. Only now start rewriting sentences.
7. Re-run the skim test.

## The limit of any template

There is no universal outline, and no prompt that makes writing coherent, because coherence emerges between writer, reader and text. The same text can be coherent to one reader and opaque to another — the words never changed, the reader did.

So the Index-Discussion structure is not a content template. It tells you **where** readers look for information, never **what** to put there. Filling the slots correctly with material the reader does not need still fails.
